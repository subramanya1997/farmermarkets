Green onions go in the refrigerator, unwashed, either in a plastic bag with a damp paper towel or standing upright in a jar with an inch or two of water. Stored that way they keep for about a week, often longer, and anything you will not get to can be chopped and frozen for cooking.

## Why Green Onions Are Not Like Other Onions

Purdue Extension's FoodLink sheet on onions describes green onions as [prized for their green leaves](https://extension.purdue.edu/foodlink/includes/pubs/Print4perpaper/4vegetablesprint/4ONION2print.pdf) and a narrow white base, sold in bunches and almost always used raw. Spring onions are close cousins with a larger bulb, and Purdue gives the same storage advice for both.

That makes them the opposite of the dry [storage onions](/blog/how-to-store-onions) and [garlic](/blog/how-to-store-garlic) that keep for weeks or months in a cool, dry place. Purdue's sheet says bulb onions can sit on the counter for up to 4 weeks, but for green onions and spring onions its advice is the fridge, with no counter option at all. They are mostly leaf, and a leaf has nothing like a papery skin to hold its moisture in.

## The Damp Paper Towel Method

Purdue and Kentucky both give some version of this method:

1. Leave the onions unwashed. The University of Kentucky's CSA program lists green onions as a vegetable to store [unwashed, in a perforated plastic bag](https://csa.mgcafe.uky.edu/resources/green-onions) in the crisper drawer.
2. Pull off any band or tie that is crushing the stems, and take out any leaves that are already slimy or yellow.
3. Wrap a damp paper towel around the root ends. Kentucky suggests exactly that: a damp towel around the ends, then the bunch into a plastic bag.
4. Put the bag in the crisper drawer.

Damp means wrung out, not dripping. The point is to keep the leaves from drying out. A bag with a few holes, or one left slightly open, lets some air move.

Purdue says green onions stored this way keep in the refrigerator for about a week. Kentucky's chart is more generous, putting the best-used-by window at 3-4 weeks. How fresh the bunch was when you bought it makes a difference you cannot see on the label, so treat a week as the plan and anything past it as a bonus.

## The Jar of Water Method

Green onions still have their roots, which means you can stand them up like cut flowers. Kentucky lists this as another option: put the onions in a jar with 1-2 inches of water, place a plastic bag over the tops, and keep the jar in the fridge.

The bag matters. Without it, the roots stay wet but the leaves dry out in the cold, dry air of the refrigerator. With it, the jar becomes a small humid chamber, which is what green onions want.

A few practical points:

- Use a jar tall enough that the bunch does not tip over. A pint mason jar or a tall drinking glass works for most bunches.
- Keep only the root ends in the water, as Kentucky's 1-2 inches suggests, not the whole stem.
- Give the jar fresh water if it starts to look cloudy, and rinse the jar while you are at it.

This takes up more shelf space than a bag in the drawer, but it also keeps the onions in sight, which is half the battle with a vegetable that gets bought for one recipe and forgotten.

## What the Professionals Do

Grocery and school kitchens store green onions colder and wetter than most home fridges run. The California Department of Education's produce guide for school nutrition programs says green onions should be stored at [32-36 degrees Fahrenheit](https://www.cde.ca.gov/LS/NU/fd/grnonion.asp) with a relative humidity of 90-98 percent, and that they should be refrigerated immediately after they arrive. It also suggests misting them lightly with water to extend storage life.

You cannot set the humidity in a home fridge, but you can get close. The crisper drawer is the most humid spot, and a bag or a jar with a bag over it holds the moisture in.

The same guide gives the short answer for an open shelf: up to five days. It also says to keep green onions away from foods that absorb odors. A bag does double duty here. It keeps the onions moist and keeps the smell off the butter, the milk and anything else in the fridge that picks up flavors.

## Choosing Green Onions at the Market

The longest life starts at the stand. The California guide describes good green onions as crisp and fresh, with tender green tops and a firm white base. Pass over bunches with limp or yellowed leaves. Purdue says not to use leaves that have shriveled or turned yellow, so a bunch that already has them has less usable onion than it looks.

For when they and everything else are at their best where you live, see [what produce is in season](/blog/what-produce-is-in-season). If you get them in a weekly box, the same rules apply; our guide to a [CSA farm share](/blog/csa-farm-share) covers how to handle a share that arrives all at once.

If you shop a [Saturday market](/markets/saturday) and want green onions all week, put them on top of the bag, not under the squash, and get them into the fridge as soon as you are home.

## Washing and Trimming

Wash green onions right before you use them, not before you store them. The FDA says to wash all produce under running water before preparing or eating it, [including produce from a farmers market](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/selecting-and-serving-produce-safely), and not to use soap, detergent or commercial produce wash. Market green onions often have soil tucked between the layers at the base, so rub that area with your fingers under the water.

Then trim. Cut off the root ends, peel away any slimy outer layer, and cut off leaves that have dried or yellowed. The FDA says to cut away damaged or bruised areas before eating produce and to throw away anything that looks rotten. A whole bunch rarely goes bad all at once; one or two soft stems can be pulled and the rest used.

Once green onions are sliced, keep them cold. The FDA says perishable produce belongs in a refrigerator at 40 degrees Fahrenheit or below. Keep sliced onions in a small covered container and slice only what you need.

## Freezing Green Onions

Freezing is the way to save a bunch you cannot finish. The National Center for Home Food Preservation says young green onions can be [chopped and frozen without blanching](https://nchfp.uga.edu/how/freeze/vegetable/freezing-onions/). It is also honest about the result: they will not be crisp, and they will be highly flavored but may be slightly tough.

So frozen green onions are for cooking: stir fries, soups, fried rice, egg dishes, anything where they go into a hot pan. For a garnish or a salad, use fresh ones.

To freeze them:

1. Wash the onions and pat them dry.
2. Slice them into rounds.
3. Pack them into a freezer bag or container and press out the air.

A bag of chopped green onions in the freezer is also a good use for the last few stems that are past their best for eating raw but not yet soft or slimy.
