SNAP is the federal food assistance program, and the benefits land each month on an EBT card you swipe like a debit card. Plenty of people assume that card is for grocery stores only. It isn't. Thousands of the markets in our [farmers market directory](/) take SNAP, and the produce you buy with it was usually picked a day or two before you got there.

## How Paying Works at the Market

A farmers market handles EBT a little differently than a supermarket checkout. Most markets that accept SNAP run one card terminal at a central information booth, sometimes called the market booth or the manager's tent. You tell the staff how much you want to spend, they run your card for that amount, and they hand back tokens, wooden coins, or paper scrip worth the same value. You then shop with those tokens the way you would shop with cash, and the market settles up with the vendors afterward.

Some vendors take EBT directly at their own stand too, especially at bigger markets. Either way the money comes out of the same balance, and there is nothing extra to sign up for. Vendors usually cannot give cash change for SNAP tokens, so it helps to swipe a smaller amount first and walk back to the booth if you want more.

## What SNAP Covers at a Market

The rules are the same ones that apply at the grocery store. Your benefits cover food you take home to cook or eat later:

- Fruit and vegetables of every kind
- Bread and baked goods meant to be eaten at home
- Meat, poultry, fish, dairy, and eggs
- Honey, jam, pickles, and other packaged foods from local producers
- **Seeds and food-producing plants**, which markets are full of in spring. A few dollars of tomato or pepper starts can feed you all summer.

Hot food and anything meant to be eaten on the spot is generally not covered, so the breakfast sandwich griddle and the coffee cart are usually cash or credit only. Non-food goods are out as well, including cut flowers, soap, candles, and crafts, and so is alcohol.

## Market Match and Other Incentive Programs

Here is the part people miss. Many markets run a nutrition incentive program that adds free money on top of what you swipe, almost always earmarked for fruit and vegetables. You will see it called Market Match, Double Up Food Bucks, or a name local to your state or county. The usual shape is simple. You swipe a certain amount in SNAP, and the booth hands you matching tokens good for produce.

The particulars vary a lot. Some programs match up to a daily cap, some run only in certain months, and some are funded season by season and stop when that funding runs out. Ask at the booth on your first visit, since terms change and the market itself will know what is running this season.

## WIC and Senior Farmers Market Nutrition Programs

Two other programs turn up at markets. In states that run it, the WIC Farmers Market Nutrition Program gives eligible WIC participants seasonal benefits for fresh fruit, vegetables, and herbs from approved farmers. The Senior Farmers Market Nutrition Program does the same for eligible older adults. Both are separate from SNAP, both are run by your state agency, and they arrive as paper checks in some states and on a card in others. Not every vendor is approved to accept them, so watch for the signs at each stand or ask the booth which farmers are signed up.

## Before You Go

- Check the listing first. Acceptance and match programs change from season to season, and the market's own website or phone number is the fastest way to confirm what is running this year.
- Stop at the information booth before you shop, not after. That is where the terminal, the tokens, and the person who knows the answers all are.
- Ask about token expiration. Many markets let SNAP tokens carry over with no expiration date, though some clear them at the end of the season, and match tokens often expire sooner than SNAP tokens.
- Confirm the day and time before you make the trip, especially if you are working around a shift or a bus schedule. You can compare [opening days and hours across the directory](/farmers-markets/hours).
