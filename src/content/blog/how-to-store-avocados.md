Keep a hard avocado on the counter until it gives a little when you press it, then put it in the refrigerator, where it holds for a few more days. Once it is cut, it has a day or two, and anything you cannot eat in time can be mashed and frozen.

## Ripen It on the Counter

Avocados do not ripen on the tree. Illinois Extension describes them as [climacteric fruit, picked green and hard and ripened after harvest](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/simply-nutritious-quick-and-delicious/2021-09-03-green-avocado-smooth-smoothie). So the rock-hard fruit at the stall is not a mistake. It is just early.

Room temperature does the work. The California Department of Education's produce guide says unripe avocados [usually ripen at 65 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ls/nu/fd/avocados.asp), and that ripening takes three to ten days depending on the time of year and on whether the fruit was treated with ethylene, the gas that triggers ripening.

A paper bag speeds things up by holding that gas near the fruit. Iowa State University's Spend Smart Eat Smart program says to [store avocados in a paper bag at room temperature to ripen](https://spendsmart.extension.iastate.edu/produce-item/avocado/), and expects it to take 2 to 5 days. A UF/IFAS Extension agent in Brevard County puts it at [a day or two in a paper bag](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/brevardco/2025/09/04/the-green-goodness-of-avocados/) for fruit that is already close. Illinois adds a perforated plastic bag or a ripening bowl as options, and says to keep the fruit out of direct sun.

None of these guides tells you to put a hard avocado in the fridge. They all ripen it first and chill it after.

## How to Tell It Is Ready

Iowa State's test is a gentle squeeze: a ripe avocado is firm but yields to gentle pressure. One that feels soft all over has gone past the point you wanted.

Color is a second clue. The Brevard County article says ripe avocados have dark green or almost black skin, while the California guide describes good avocados as green or purple-black, so on a green-skinned variety the squeeze tells you more than the color.

At the market, the California guide says to pass over fruit with bruises or dented areas. Iowa State says to look for firm skin with no soft spots. Buy a mix if you can: one or two that give a little for tonight, and a few hard ones that will come ready over the next week.

## Refrigerate It Once It Is Ripe

A ripe avocado on the counter turns quickly. The Brevard County article calls it a narrow window of ripeness, and the fridge is how you widen it.

How long you get depends on whose guide you read:

- Illinois Extension: ripe, whole avocados keep about 1 to 3 days in the refrigerator.
- California Department of Education: refrigerate ripe fruit you are not eating and use it within 2 to 3 days.
- Iowa State: a ripe, uncut avocado keeps 3 to 4 days in the refrigerator.

Call it two or three days and treat anything past that as a bonus. Leave the fruit whole and uncut until you need it. The FDA's general advice for perishable produce is to keep it in [a clean refrigerator at 40 degrees Fahrenheit or below](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/selecting-and-serving-produce-safely), and it suggests a fridge thermometer to check.

The practical trick is staggering. If you buy six hard avocados on a Saturday, they will not all ripen on the same day, so check them each morning and move each one to the fridge as it softens. That spreads one market trip across a week or more.

## Wash It Before You Cut It

You do not eat the skin, so washing an avocado feels pointless. It is not. The FDA sampled 1,615 whole avocados and found Listeria monocytogenes on [64 of the 361 skin samples it tested, or 17.73 percent](https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-releases-reports-avocado-and-hot-pepper-sampling), against 3 of 1,254 pulp samples. A knife carries whatever is on the skin straight into the flesh.

The FDA's produce guidance says to wash produce under running water even when you will not eat the skin, so dirt and bacteria are not carried in when you peel or cut it. It recommends a clean produce brush for firm produce and drying with a clean cloth or paper towel afterward. It also says not to use soap, detergent or commercial produce wash, since produce can absorb them. Iowa State gives the short version: rinse the avocado under cool running water before cutting.

## Storing a Cut Avocado

Cut flesh turns brown where the air reaches it. The Brevard County article notes that the browning does not affect the taste, but it does not look good on toast.

Acid slows it down. Iowa State says to sprinkle the cut surface with lemon juice, lime juice or white vinegar, and Illinois says to do the same and keep it in an airtight container in the refrigerator.

Both give it a short life. Iowa State says cut avocados last 1 to 2 days in the refrigerator, and Illinois says ideally use it in a day or two.

Guacamole is cut avocado with the acid already in it, so give it the same airtight container and the same day or two.

## Freezing Avocados

The freezer method the extension guides give is for mashed avocado, not halves or slices. Colorado State University's Preserve Smart guide lays out [the steps for freezing avocado puree](https://apps.chhs.colostate.edu/preservesmart/produce/freezing/avocados):

1. Start with soft, ripe avocados, with no decay or mold.
2. Wash them with the skin on, then peel, halve, pit and mash the flesh.
3. Add 1/8 teaspoon of ascorbic acid (vitamin C powder) for each quart of puree, or 1 tablespoon of lemon juice for every 2 avocados, to keep the color.
4. Pack it tightly into containers. In a wide-mouth container, leave 1/2 inch of headspace in a pint and 1 inch in a quart. In a freezer bag, press out as much air as you can.
5. Label it with the date and freeze it at 0 degrees Fahrenheit or below, in a single layer so it freezes quickly.

The same guide says most fruits keep their best quality for 8 to 12 months at 0 degrees or below. Thaw it in the refrigerator and use it where texture does not matter much: the Brevard County article suggests adding frozen avocado to smoothies, and a puree also works in dips and dressings.

## Where Avocados Come From

Avocados grow where winters are mild, so you will see them from local growers at markets in parts of [California](/california) and [Florida](/florida), and less often elsewhere. Outside those places, ask the vendor where the avocados were grown if buying local is the point of your trip. Our guide to [what produce is in season](/blog/what-produce-is-in-season) covers how to find the harvest calendar for your area and what to ask the grower.

Storing avocados is mostly the opposite of storing [garlic](/blog/how-to-store-garlic), which wants a dry pantry shelf for months. An avocado wants the counter for a few days, the fridge for a few more, and the freezer if you bought too many.
