A farmers market is better for produce picked close to ripe, for varieties you will never see in a supermarket, and for asking the person who grew it how it was grown. A grocery store is better for staples, for shopping at nine on a Tuesday night, and for buying the same thing at the same price every week. Most people who shop at both stop treating it as a contest and split the list.

## Freshness and Ripeness

The biggest difference is time. A supermarket tomato has to survive packing, a truck, a distribution center and a few days on a shelf, so it is often picked before it is fully ripe, and the varieties are chosen partly for how well they ship. A tomato at a market stall usually only had to survive the drive in that morning, so the grower can pick it ripe and grow varieties that would bruise in a shipping box.

That gap is widest for things that go downhill fast after harvest: sweet corn, berries, peaches, tomatoes, salad greens and herbs. It is smallest for things that store well anyway, like onions, potatoes, winter squash and apples in the months after harvest. If you only change where you buy a handful of items, start with the fragile ones.

Freshness at a market is not automatic, though. Some stands resell wholesale produce alongside their own, and a hot afternoon can wilt greens that were perfect at eight. Ask when something was picked and whether they grew it. Most growers like the question, and a vague answer tells you something too.

## Price

There is no simple rule here. Market prices are set by small farms with small volumes, so some things cost more than at the supermarket: early strawberries, specialty greens, pasture-raised eggs and meat. Other things cost the same or less, especially at the peak of a crop's season when every stand has too many tomatoes or zucchini.

A few habits keep the bill down:

- Compare by the pound, not by the basket. A quart box and a pint box look alike from a few feet away.
- Buy what is in heavy supply that week. Our guide to [what produce is in season](/blog/what-produce-is-in-season) explains how to tell what is peaking where you live.
- Ask about seconds, the split or oddly shaped pieces sold cheap for sauce, jam or the freezer.
- Show up late for the occasional end-of-day discount, and early if you need something that sells out.

Where the money goes is different too. The USDA Economic Research Service estimates that farmers received [18.5 cents of each dollar spent on food eaten at home in 2024](https://ers.usda.gov/data-products/food-dollar/summary-findings), with the rest going to processing, packaging, transport, retail and other costs. At a market stall you are paying the grower directly, so far more of that dollar stays on the farm, minus whatever the market charges for the space.

## What "Organic" Means at Each

At the grocery store, organic is easy to read: look for the USDA organic seal. At a market, it takes a question or two.

Under USDA rules, farms that sell more than $5,000 of organic products a year must be certified to call them organic. Farms below that line are exempt from certification but still have to follow the organic production rules if they use the word, according to the USDA's fact sheet on [organic labeling at farmers markets](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/Organic%20at%20Farmers%20Markets.pdf). An exempt farm may say "organic" but may not put the USDA organic seal on its products or call them certified organic, per the USDA's [guide to who needs to be certified](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/DoINeedTobeCertifiedOrganicFactSheet.pdf).

Plenty of small growers farm with few or no synthetic sprays and never certify, because the paperwork and fees are hard to justify at their size. When a sign says "no spray" or "we don't use pesticides," ask what that means in practice: no sprays at all, or only certain ones, and on which crops. If a vendor says they are certified, you can look the farm up in the USDA's [Organic Integrity Database](https://organic.ams.usda.gov/integrity/), which lists certified operations by name and location.

## Food Safety

Neither place hands you produce that is ready to eat without a rinse. The FDA's [tips for cleaning fruits and vegetables](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/7-tips-cleaning-fruits-vegetables) apply the same way to a bag from either one: rinse produce before you peel it, rub it under plain running water, skip soap and produce washes, scrub firm things like melons and cucumbers with a clean brush, and dry it with a clean towel.

Market produce often comes with more field dirt on it, since it has not been through a packing line. That is a reason to rinse well, not a sign it is less safe. The same FDA page says to keep pre-cut produce refrigerated or on ice, which matters on a warm morning if you buy cut melon and then keep shopping for an hour.

## Convenience, Hours and Paying

This is where the grocery store wins outright. It is open every day, usually late, and it carries everything from rice to dish soap. Most farmers markets run one or two days a week for a few hours, and many close for the winter. You can check [market days and opening hours](/markets/hours) before you plan around one, or look for [markets that stay open year-round](/markets/year-round) if you want a winter option.

Payment is the other practical gap. Most supermarkets take cards and SNAP right at the register. At a market, many vendors take cards now but some are cash only, and SNAP is often handled at the market's information booth rather than at each stand. Of the 9,143 markets listed on this site, 1,791 are marked as accepting SNAP, so check a market's listing or the list of [markets that take SNAP, WIC or FMNP](/markets/snap-ebt) before you count on it.

The upside is that some markets stretch SNAP further than a grocery store does. The USDA's food assistance agency describes [incentive programs at farmers markets](https://www.fns.usda.gov/snap/farmers-markets) that give SNAP shoppers matching dollars, as tokens or paper coupons, for fruits and vegetables. Our guide to [using SNAP and EBT at farmers markets](/blog/how-to-use-snap-ebt-at-farmers-markets) covers how the booth and the matching work.

## How to Split the List

A simple way to use both:

- **Buy at the market:** whatever is in season and fragile, like tomatoes, berries, stone fruit, sweet corn, salad greens and herbs. Also eggs, honey, and anything you want to ask the grower about.
- **Buy at the store:** pantry staples, dairy if your market does not carry it, bananas, citrus out of season, and anything you need on a day the market is closed.
- **Either one:** storage crops like onions, potatoes and winter squash. Price them both ways for a few weeks and go with whichever wins where you live.

If you want market produce without planning a Saturday around it, a farm share delivers a box every week through the season; our guide to [how CSA farm shares work](/blog/csa-farm-share) covers what to ask before you sign up. And if you have not been to a market before, start with [your first visit to a farmers market](/blog/first-visit-to-a-farmers-market) for what to bring and when to go.
